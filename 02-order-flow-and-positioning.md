# Order flow and positioning

Positioning claims rest on a handful of measurable things: volume, open interest, order flow, and off-exchange prints. Each measures something narrow, and treating any one as a window into intent overstates what it shows.

## Volume

Volume is the count of shares or contracts traded in a period. High volume means activity, not direction. A stock can rise or fall on the same volume. Volume confirms that something happened, not who did it or why.

## Open interest

Open interest is the number of derivative contracts currently outstanding. Rising open interest with rising price is often read as new positioning, but open interest does not record whether a contract was opened by a buyer expressing a view or a dealer hedging. The same number is consistent with opposite stories.

## Order flow

Order flow describes the sequence and size of buy and sell orders hitting the market. Some services classify trades as buyer- or seller-initiated using the price relative to the bid and ask. This classification is an estimate, not a fact, and it says nothing about the motive behind the order.

## Off-exchange prints and dark pools

A large share of volume executes away from the lit exchanges, in venues often called dark pools. These prints appear in the public record after the fact, without identifying the participant or the reason. A large off-exchange print is frequently described as institutional accumulation. It might be. It might also be an index rebalance, a hedge, a tax trade, or a transfer. The print alone does not distinguish these.

## The common thread

Every one of these measures is downstream of a decision you cannot see. They tell you that activity occurred and roughly where. They do not tell you intent. Most overconfident positioning claims come from treating an estimate of activity as a statement of intent.
