# What the terms mean

"Retail" and "smart money" are categories of convenience, not precise groups. Knowing what each label points at, and how fuzzy the boundary is, prevents most of the bad reasoning that follows from them.

## Retail

Retail refers to individual investors trading their own money, usually in smaller size, through ordinary brokerage accounts. The defining traits people attach to retail are smaller position sizes, less access to data and tooling, and a tendency to trade on news after it is already public. None of these are universal. Plenty of individuals trade large, well-researched positions, and plenty of professionals trade badly.

## Institutional

Institutional refers to entities investing pooled or mandated capital: pension funds, mutual funds, hedge funds, insurance portfolios, endowments, and market makers. They trade in larger size, often have research staff and direct data feeds, and are subject to disclosure rules that individuals are not. Institutional is a real, definable category because it maps to legal entity types and reporting requirements.

## Smart money

"Smart money" is the slippery one. It is not a legal category or a data field. It is a story people tell about a subset of institutional or experienced participants assumed to be better informed. The assumption is sometimes true and often not. A large fund can be wrong, early, hedging, or forced to act for reasons unrelated to a view on price. Treating "smart money" as a single well-informed actor is the first and most common mistake.

## Why the line blurs

The same trade can be retail or institutional depending on who routes it. A market maker holding a large position may be hedging client flow, not expressing a directional view. A fund's reported holding may be months stale by the time you see it. When you read "smart money is positioned for X," ask which entities, measured how, as of when. Usually the honest answer weakens the claim considerably.
