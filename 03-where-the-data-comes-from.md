# Where the data comes from

Public positioning data has real sources, and each one has a lag or a blind spot that limits how much you can conclude. Knowing the source and its limit is the difference between informed reading and guesswork.

## Regulatory filings

Large institutional managers file periodic reports of their holdings. These are useful for seeing what a manager held at a point in the past. The limit is the lag: filings can be weeks to months old when published, and they typically show long positions, not shorts, options, or what has changed since. A filing tells you where someone was, not where they are.

## Exchange and consolidated tape data

Exchanges publish volume, price, and open interest. Consolidated feeds aggregate trades across venues. This is the most reliable category for raw activity, but it carries no identity and no intent. You see the trade, never the trader's reason.

## Derivatives and clearing data

Aggregated reports on derivatives positioning group participants into broad categories. These are valuable for seeing how categories of participant are leaning in aggregate, with the caveat that category definitions are coarse and a single report cannot separate a directional bet from a hedge.

## Vendor estimates

Many popular metrics are vendor estimates built on top of the raw feeds: trade classification, implied positioning, sentiment scores. These can be reasonable, but they are models with assumptions, not measurements. Treat a vendor number as one analyst's estimate, because that is what it is.

## The honest summary

The cleanest data has no intent attached. The data that claims intent is estimated or lagged. There is no public feed that shows you what informed money is doing right now and why. Anyone implying otherwise is selling the implication.
